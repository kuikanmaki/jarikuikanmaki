SUMMARY

Document Origins, Methodology & Background

The findings that follow are based on a trove of EcoHealth Alliance (EHA) documents effectively

made public by Congress, with the help of a total absence of access restrictions by EHA. These

materials include over 25,000 calendar items of Peter Daszak, internal correspondence, and

project folders—some of which EHA flagged as “not to be shared with Congress”. The materials

were explored and archived by DRASTIC researchers before public access was eventually

suspended.

The findings offer a navigation guide through this substantial trove, and it is hoped that they may

provide reference points for future investigators (who may ask for full access). The authors also

emphasise the ethical handling of private data, even if effectively disclosed by Congress, choosing

not to publicise irrelevant personal information found in the folders.

Key Projects, Proposals & Timelines

These findings highlight the ambitious but also opportunistic and chaotic management of projects,

samples, and regulatory constraints, under Peter Daszak. The findings also show how, once under

Congressional scrutiny, EHA tried to compensate for these issues by a razor-sharp focus on public

relations and political lobbying, and how EHA then attempted to ignore the requests from the

intelligence community, or—in the case of the FBI—tried to shape their perceptions by playing the

victim and by deflecting their attention towards its critics.

Much of the early focus of these findings is on EHA’s participation in US government biological

threat reduction projects (DTRA/BTRP), before moving to the controversial DEFUSE proposal. We

learn that Peter Daszak was in talks with DARPA’s Dr. Jim Gimlett as early as June 2017, visiting him

with Shi Zhengli at the time. Feedback from Daszak and Zhengli likely influenced the subsequent

design of the DARPA PREEMPT program by Dr. Gimlett; a program that Daszak and Zhengli would

then answer with DEFUSE in early 2018.

The DEFUSE proposal process itself was fraught with rushed deadlines and technical missteps. Key

risk-mitigation plans required by DARPA—addressing issues like GoF, DURC (Dual Use Research of

Concern), and ELSI (Ethical, Legal, and Societal Implications)—were handled so carelessly as to be

accidentally omitted in the final proposal due to some last-minute editing error. That omission

proved crucial, as it was a major contribution to DEFUSE’s rejection.

A more detailed proposal of Baric intended contribution, drafted by himself, is also available for

the first time. It shows more emphasis on passaging and the FCS than the earlier version known to

this date. The findings lastly show how, after DEFUSE’s rejection, EHA attempted to recycle

aspects of DEFUSE for pared-down proposals in response to emerging COVID-19 concerns.

to counter moves in Congress aiming to cut its funding, including high-profile amendments to US

This document covers the political and scientific fallout around notable publications and

Latinne et al.(Nat. Comms) receives a lot of attention too, due to the documented

Global Virome Project (GVP)

Efforts to secure lasting DTRA/USAID funding post the programmed end of PREDICT-2 are a constant

undercurrent in this trove of documents. While DEFUSE was a much more limited program that

anyway met an early death, these findings shed more light on the GVP, the ambitious effort to

catalog global viral threats conceived by Carroll and Daszak specifically as a way to ensure ample

regular funding for 10 years. The findings offer new materials on the genesis of the GVP, the

expected role of China, and the way the State Department was being tapped for support.

USAID and DoD funding

This document shows how EHA was able to largely make up for the interruption of its NIH grant

through its usual backers (and real masters): the DoD and USAID. Large USAID grants were

showered on EHA, largely in places that were not in the investigative limelight, such as Liberia,

India, Jordan, Tanzania, Guinea, and the Philippines.

Final Notes

The authors note that this findings document is not exhaustive due to the sheer volume of data

now available for further journalistic or academic examination. Serious journalists are invited to

ask for full access.

Our working notes:

This document is a compendium of notes we took while going through the folder. Given that it is very easy to

get lost in the many files in that folder, we decided to write our findings as a form of guidance for ourselves

and for further investigators. Our findings are in no particular order and cannot be expected to be exhaustive

given the size of that document stash.

In these findings, we often refer to Daszak’s calendar items, so it may be useful to explain their formatting.

The approximately 26,000 calendar items were in an ICS (iCalendar) file. To allow for easy filtering and proper

reading, we converted it to csv, loaded it up in a spreadsheet, and fixed some fields (dates, broken HTML often

resulting from cutting and pasting content from key emails in the event description, etc.). We then

reformatted each clean calendar entry for convenient display via an HTML viewer.

Please note that we removed most of the links in this document. If you are a journalist, you may ask us for

access to the folder (contact: PihaBeach@proton.me) and for a version of this document with working links to

the elements in that folder. If we agree to share these with you, it shall be with the expectation that no

personal data irrelevant to the origin questions shall be released, as a matter of courtesy, even if the full

documents were made freely accessible for many weeks by Congress itself.

FBI, CIA and DOE subfolders:

We want it to be clear that the CIA and DOE subfolders (07 and 08) were empty, which is likely better that way.

The FBI folder was not. But far from containing remarkable revelations, it just exposes how Daszak immediately

tried to manipulate his FBI Special Agent contact, portraying himself as a hero and a victim, while

recommending the names of various regular people, investigators, and even some perfectly sane reporter, to

his special attention. As it is, the DRASTIC team also got a few mentions.

Authors and timing:

This document was largely compiled by Gilles DEMANEUF, with some help from Billy BOSTICKSON. We were both

quite busy over the last 8 months and were also in no rush to publish our findings as the US government

transition was underway. We thus took our time before making these summary notes public.

7.EHA: Lobbying to fight defunding threats

7.1.NDAA, HASC and SASC

● The National Defense Authorization Act (NDAA) provides authorization of appropriations for the

Department of Defense (DOD), nuclear weapons programs of the Department of Energy, and other

defense-related activities. The process starts around early February and takes 8 months. The House

Committee on Armed Services (also known as the House Armed Services Committee, or HASC) and the

Senate Committee on Armed Services (also known as the Senate Armed Services Committee, or SASC)

have jurisdiction, and scrutinize the President's budget request. The House and the Senate must then

approve the same bill after reconciliation of their differences before the end of September. That bill is

then presented to the President

● As the House and the Senate try to pull the final bill their way, the final version ends up watering down

or altogether dropping some of the non-consensual wording initially proposed by the Senate or the

House, and often trades narrow-interest amendments (‘log-rolling’). This offers plenty of time for

successful lobbying.
